Buddha’s Teachings: a Communist understanding

Buddhism is one of the most prominent philosophies of life on earth. Inside Buddhism are also many philosophies that developed later after the death of Buddha. I want to understand Buddhism from my own point of view. I have not yet deeply read Buddhist teachings or books. I am just trying to analyse his initial life.

Gautama Siddhartha was born to high class family in their tribe. His story as buddha starts from when he went out of his comfortable home. Outside he witnessed misery. He saw poor people, ill people, miserable people left on their own. He was moved by these scenes and decided to leave his comfortable life to seek for the truth. After leaving his home, his wife, his son, and wandered south meditating. After many years of meditation and dhyaan he was able to achieve enlightenment. Which he then shared with other monks around him.

We are told his teachings were to remove ourselves from the world and materialistic life. To not involve is to not get hurt. To not be hurt is to not experience pain and sufferings.

I question whether he became Buddha at the enlightenment or at the moment he shows compassion for the needy. I so wonder why we start there when we talk about him. When he was travelling the grounds, he often had many companions who meditated around him. So why only Gautama? I also question, why was this such a turn in his life. Where people then just not compassionate? Was it so shocking at the time or times when Buddhism was growing?

As a child I believed that he must have seen misery first time in his life and hence it was such a sharp turn. To have left his home, his family, and the people he had under him, it must have been for the very first time he ever had seen such atrocities. Because if he had seen them before he would have left even earlier to seek the end of them. Because I as a child could not comprehend anyone looking at the needy and not feel any pain inside. I still cannot not feel pain.

But he was the son of a chieftain. He was old enough to be married and have his own son. How could it be that he had seen them for the first time? It was almost half a century BCE. Could the royal people then have such resources to maintain such a huge personal space free of miserable people, such that a prince and his family could have comfortably lived in there for over 20 years?

I think this was because back then and for a significant time afterwards, people had lost the ability to feel compassion in that society. Which is why the Grand Renunciation was the start of Siddhartha becoming Buddha. The teachings of Buddha are not just about getting rid of suffering, but also acknowledging suffering. Not just for oneself but for the society. What I take from this is that one must treat others pain like their own, that the individual is not in isolation. The pain in society will eventually flow through you too.

Another question I had about these events was, why did not Gautama use his influential position in the society to erase the pain of others? When Gautama had enough food, why did he not share it with them? When Gautama had firm bed, why did he not extend it towards the sick? When we see someone lacking, our first solution is to give. Why did not Gautama? Maybe he had and was unsuccessful? But how would have Great Renunciation help?

Here I have a more communistic view of society. Now and then. The bourgeoisie, and the proletariat. In a capitalistic society, proletariat creates labour but does not own it. Bourgeoisie takes the labour, over the reasoning that the resources through which labour was created were their property, and defines who gains what percentage of what they produced.

Shakya clan, the clan where Gautama was born, is today understood to be an Oligarchic Republican. I am not aware of the political structure, but I would still try to look at it through my communistic view.

What options does a singular Proletariat have to escape this stealing of labour? Almost none. What about a big enough group? First escape the current system, then create their own system. Buddhas renunciation seems similar. He escaped the system. But he was not a Proletariat? Why did he renunciate? He owned the resources. He could have just redistributed them? So why not stay?

My answer is that he knew how the system worked. If he gave up his assets, they would flow to the bourgeoisie again. If he, for his compassion, was not strictly fulfilling his role of bourgeoisie, he would be forcefully turned into the proletariat. Instead of removing the misery, he would have joined it. Hence, the path he chose was to show the path of escape, of renunciation, to everyone else. A system which is not just, is not acceptable to be in. Suffering came from the desire of companionship of an unjust system. Suffering comes from materialistic life, which is a characteristic of an unjust society. Suffering comes from an unjust society. And to end this cycle of suffering, we must bring injustice to an end, by renunciation of the unjust system.

The story of Sujata is also of importance in my view. Because she showed Buddha that total removal of system was not the answer. That his earlier assumption that any and all interactions bring suffering was wrong. Interaction and society are required for a healthy and fruitful life. And there are ways to interact without causing each other suffering. That is the society we all should move towards, after escaping the unjust one.
